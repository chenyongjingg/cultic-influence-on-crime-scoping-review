## Abstract

**Background.** High-control groups sit at the junction of social psychology, group dynamics and criminal conduct. Mental manipulation, coercive control and indoctrination shape how courts and clinicians attribute agency, and therefore findings of criminal responsibility. The relation between these mechanisms and criminal outcomes is widely discussed and rarely compared. **Objective.** To map what the literature on cultic influence and crime demonstrates, what it measures and what it leaves unreported, and to derive a minimum reporting set from that map. **Methods.** Scoping review following PRISMA-ScR and JBI guidance, specified before searching. Six bibliographic sources were searched from 1994 to November 2026 with a base block requiring a high-control-group term and a crime term in title or abstract. Records were screened against four criteria by a fixed open-weight language model, with every verdict, supporting span and confidence retained; reporting completeness was then measured by twelve documented lexical detectors, each returning its matched span so that every count can be re-derived. No effect size was pooled and no significance test was computed. **Results.** 191 records met the inclusion criteria, 170 of which carried an abstract; full text was obtainable for 42 of them (24.7%). Among the measurable abstracts, 5.9% stated how the group was operationally identified, 14.7% distinguished participant roles, 4.1% reported involvement or exit duration, 1.2% named a standardised instrument and 20.6% reported a legal outcome. The evidence map is sparse. Most mechanism–outcome cells are empty. **Conclusions.** The variables that carry the causal claim — role, duration, instrument, legal outcome — are the least reported in the abstract, but they are not missing from the papers. A paired reading of abstracts against full texts raises the standardised-instrument rate from 0% to 28.6%, and the legal-outcome rate from 16.7% to 66.7%. The deficit sits at the reading interface rather than in the scholarship, and a ten-item minimum reporting set is proposed for that interface, framed as a disclosure instrument a regulator could impose at low cost.

## 1. Introduction

A cult, in the sense this review uses, is not a belief system but an organisation
that constrains exit. The distinction matters legally: what a court needs to know
is not whether a doctrine is eccentric but whether a person's capacity to choose
was systematically narrowed, by whom, over what period, and with what consequence.
That question sits at the junction of three literatures that do not usually read
each other — the social psychology of influence, the criminology of deviant groups,
and the forensic assessment of responsibility.

Each literature answers a part of it. Social psychology has produced the mechanism
vocabulary: coercive control, thought reform, information control, trauma bonding,
groupthink, obedience. Criminology has produced the outcome vocabulary: offence
types, victim selection, organisational structure, network concealment. Forensic
psychology has produced the instruments: structured professional judgement tools,
risk frameworks, and the general apparatus of criminal responsibility assessment.
What has not been produced, at least not in a form that can be audited, is the
bridge: a body of work in which a named mechanism is measured in a comparable way
and set against a named criminal or judicial outcome.

This absence is not for want of cases. Jonestown, Aum Shinrikyo, the Manson family,
the Order of the Solar Temple and a long tail of smaller groups supply an abundant
case record, and case-based scholarship on them is substantial. The gap is
structural rather than empirical. Case material establishes that a mechanism can
operate; it cannot establish how often, in whom, against which outcome, because
each case supplies its own definitions, its own measures and its own vocabulary.
When a literature is built this way the consequence is a reading problem as much as
an evidentiary one: nothing in a case report obliges it to surface, in a form another
study can use, who the person was in the group, how long they were there, or what the
court decided. The field can display mechanisms; assembling those displays into a
comparable body of work is the step that does not happen.

Prior syntheses exist, and none is a systematic map. The closest is an editorial
handbook for forensic practitioners, which integrates theory, clinical and legal
material in narrative form and states that systematic research in this area is
scarce [1]. The only review focused on violent outcomes is seventeen years old
and restricted to violence [2]. Work on joining and leaving
examines recruitment and exit rather than offending [3]. A systematic review of
coercive control exists, but it is anchored in intimate partner violence rather than
in groups [4]. Two studies established that the cult–crime intersection is
empirically reachable [5,6]. None of them supplies a reproducible corpus, a
measure of reporting completeness, or a reporting standard.

That consequence is the object of this review, and it is measurable. If the
comparison problem is real, it should show up as a specific pattern of reporting:
the variables that make comparison possible — who the person was in the group, how
long they were there, which instrument measured what, what the court decided —
should be the least frequently reported. Variables that carry no comparative work,
such as the name of the group or a narrative of the mechanism, should be reported
almost universally. The pattern is a testable prediction about the corpus, and this
review tests it by counting.

Four contributions follow. First, the reading-interface finding: the quantities that
make studies comparable are in the papers and absent from their abstracts, on a paired
comparison with a length-matched control. Second, a comparative map across twelve
constructs, each reported as n/N with its denominator. Third, a map of the
investigative record and what it does and does not contain (Section 3.5). Fourth, the
regulatory asymmetry: every instrument the corpus names is ex post, none operates on
information, and MRS-CI is proposed as a disclosure-based instrument of low burden.
The binding constraint on this field is the comparability of what studies report.

The review is scoping rather than systematic-with-meta-analysis, and this is a
substantive position. The mechanism variables are measured with different
instruments, in different legal systems, on data of different provenance. Pooling
them would estimate the pooled distribution of measurement conventions, not the
effect of manipulation on crime. PRISMA-ScR and the JBI guidance accommodate
exactly this situation: descriptive statistics and qualitative content analysis,
no pooled estimate, no significance test.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

Its conduct was specified before searching: population, concept and context; the
search strategy; the eligibility criteria; the screening and extraction procedures;
and three guiding questions with their anticipated judgements. The PRISMA-ScR
checklist [7] is submitted with the manuscript and the JBI
updated guidance [8] was followed for conduct and reporting.
Deviations from the protocol, and the constraint that forced each, are listed in
Supplementary Table S1; they are summarised where they bear on interpretation.

### 2.2 Eligibility

**Population.** People involved in high-control groups — leaders, core executors,
ordinary members, victims, and former members — and the empirical studies, case
reports, forensic assessments and court documents built on them. The unit of analysis is the record.

**Concept.** The relation between a psychological manipulation mechanism
(indoctrination, thought reform, coercive control, mind control, social isolation,
trauma bonding, groupthink, cognitive dissonance, information control, obedience)
and a criminal or judicial outcome, together with the completeness with which the
mechanism and the outcome are reported.

**Context.** Published, peer-reviewed, legal and grey literature in English, from
1994 — the year the Group Psychological Abuse scale was published [9] and the point from which empirical work in this area becomes continuous —
to the search date.

Four criteria were applied to titles and abstracts: **I1** the record concerns a
cult or high-control group as an actual organised group; **I2** it concerns criminal
conduct or the criminal-justice process; **I3** a manipulation mechanism is
analysed rather than merely named; **I4** it carries empirical or documentary
material. The four are conjunctive. I1 is what excludes the large metaphorical
literature — "cult" films, the "cult of the double-blind trial" — that any keyword
search on this topic retrieves in quantity, and I2 is what implements the target
topic's own boundary, which excludes non-criminal cult activity.

### 2.3 Sources and search

Six sources were searched. Crossref (87 records passing the base
block), Semantic Scholar (410), CORE (269), Europe PMC
(46 in the primary tier, plus a widened tier), PubMed (49)
and DOAJ (150) together retrieved 1154 records before
deduplication.

The choice of sources follows a constraint. OpenAlex, the protocol's original primary source, moved in February 2026 to credit
metering; its free allowance was exhausted during pre-search development and every
request returned HTTP 429 with a retry-after of about eighteen hours. The search was
rebuilt on sources measurable on the execution date, and their yields differ. CORE
returned 3,046 hits for the composite query but its unkeyed quota allowed
269 records before persistent throttling; Crossref ranks by relevance and
yields few on-topic records per thousand rows; Semantic Scholar served 410
records only after repeated 429 backoff. The biomedical sources remain the weakest fit
for a criminological and legal literature, which is itself a finding about where this
field publishes.

Grey literature followed the same rule. Three policy documents inform the
background — the Victorian Parliament committee report on harm in high-control groups,
the Family Survival Trust report on coercive control in UK cultic groups, and the
McIvor et al. review — and none entered the corpus: none is peer-reviewed and none
reports a codable mechanism–outcome pair. They are cited as context, not counted.

The search used a base block plus five nested blocks. The base block requires at
least one high-control-group term and at least one crime term in the title or
abstract; the nested blocks add manipulation-mechanism terms, offence-type terms,
measurement terms, forensic and legal terms, and intervention and exit terms, and
are reported as filters with their own counts. The full strategy, per-source counts, retrieval dates and artefact
hashes are in Supplementary Table S2.

### 2.4 Screening

Records were screened against I1–I4 by a fixed open-weight language model running
locally on one RTX 4090, one record per prompt, greedy decoding, fixed seed. The
production screen used Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct at 4-bit AWQ; a 7B model
(Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) screened the same records for the model-agreement experiment
E5. For each criterion the model returned a verdict together with a verbatim span
from the record, and only then a decision and a confidence; the order is part of the
method, because in the pilot the model answered "everything is irrelevant" when the
decision field came first and located evidence correctly when the span came first.

Three gates convert the model's output into an inclusion decision, and each is computed from the output. First, the decision is
computed from the four criterion flags, not read from the model's own decision
field: the pilot caught the model returning INCLUDE while one of its own criteria
was false. Second, the group criterion is conjunctive — a record must carry a
lexical group anchor (cult, sect, high-control group, new religious movement,
destructive cult, charismatic group, commune) *and* receive a positive I1 verdict.
The lexical half exists because the model treated intimate partner violence,
commercial sexual exploitation and a nerve-agent attack as cult contexts; the
semantic half exists because a title can contain the word without the topic.
Third, automatic exclusion requires EXCLUDE with confidence of at least 0.90, and is
overridden for any record whose own title carries both a group term and a crime
term.

A validation read tested this. Fifty records — twenty-five included and
twenty-five auto-excluded — were read against the four criteria. Of the twenty-five
inclusions, 11 were clearly on topic, 5 were
arguable and 9 were not: the errors are
records where "cult" is a metaphor (veneration, fashion, film criticism) or where a
criminal organisation is not a high-control group. Of the twenty-five auto-exclusions,
23 were correctly excluded and 2 were arguably retrievable, both legal analyses
sitting at the cult/crime boundary. Two consequences follow, and both are carried into
the reading of the results. First, the 9 non-qualifying records are not inert
denominator: they report a mean of 1.67 constructs
against a corpus mean of 2.08, and
3 of them report a jurisdiction, which is
one of the two largest bars in Figure 3. Removing them would move offence type from
48.8% to 48.4% and
jurisdiction from 52.4% to
53.4% — in both directions, and by about a
percentage point. The
profile therefore does not depend on the screen's precision, but the direction of the
error is not the one that would flatter the paper: the corpus is not biased towards
over-reporting, and it is not biased towards under-reporting either. Second, no
precision claim is made for the screen; the sample is small, the reader is the executing agent, not an independent reviewer, and the operative check will be the
author adjudication of the full table, which has not yet been performed.
Supplementary Table S7 gives the verdicts record by record.

Two disclosures belong here. No double
independent screening by two human reviewers was performed; what replaces the
inter-rater statistic is the full decision trail, a prompt-perturbation re-run that
measures how far decisions move when the criterion wording is paraphrased, and a
count of how many auto-excluded records a more permissive reading would have
rescued. These are stability and sensitivity, and none of them is an estimate of
human agreement. The second pass was rule-based rather than human: adjudication by
the named authors remains outstanding, and the numbers here are the machine record,
not a claim of human review. In the pilot the model's residual errors were
concentrated in one direction — it over-admitted intimate-partner-violence records
before the I1 gate was made explicit, and over-rejected when asked to judge twelve
constructs at once — and both are recorded in the repository's deviation log. The
validation sample will be read blind by a second author, and the two readings will be
reported side by side in Supplementary Table S7, completed together with the author
adjudication in Table S3.

### 2.5 Measurement of reporting completeness

Reporting completeness was measured at the level of title and abstract, over twelve
constructs: the ten items of the minimum reporting set (Table 2), plus sample size
and comparison. Each construct was measured by a
documented detector — a term list, a numeric pattern or a two-condition rule, with
the full set given in the repository and summarised in Supplementary Table S2. A
construct counts as *reported* when its detector fires, and the detector returns the
matched span, which is reproduced in the extraction table for every record so that
any reader can inspect the basis of any flag and can disagree with the term list and
recount.

The detectors were themselves validated, because everything in Section 3.4 rests on
them. Thirty records drawn at random were coded by hand against the same twelve
constructs, blind to the detector output, and the two readings compared. The
detectors are precise and conservative: overall precision 0.80, overall recall 0.45,
with recall uneven across constructs — 0.62 for a named mechanism, 0.60 for
jurisdiction, 0.52 for offence type, 0.45 for legal outcome, but only 0.24 for a group
operational definition and 0.25 for participant role. It means
the reported percentages are lower bounds, and that the *distance* between a
high-recall and a low-recall construct is partly a measurement effect rather than a
property of the literature. Where the argument depends on the ordering, the paper
therefore leans on the comparison that survives the correction: the two least
reported constructs are named in 1.2% and 2.4% of records
(standardised instrument, Table 2; offence severity), and even if their recall matched the best-observed
construct (0.62) they would rise only to about 1.9% and 3.9% — still an order of
magnitude below offence type and jurisdiction. The same
read gives precision by construct between 0.73 and 1.00 where it can be estimated,
so the error runs in the direction of under-counting, not over-counting.

A language-model extractor was implemented first and abandoned: on a pilot of 28
records it marked all twelve constructs absent for abstracts that plainly named an
offence type and a jurisdiction, and its verbatim anchors failed a string re-check in
30% of the cases where it did fire. A lexical detector measures *whether the words are
there*, and that is exactly the claim the reporting set is about.

The obvious alternative was tested. The same 29 hand-coded records were put to the
14B model one construct at a time — the prompt form that works for it — and scored
against the hand read: precision 0.846, recall
0.177, rising to 0.62 only for a named mechanism. It would have
reported offence type in 2 of 29 records where the hand read finds it in all 29. An
instrument right when it fires and silent most of the time cannot carry a prevalence
estimate, which is why the measurement layer is lexical.

What is measured is what a record states at its reading interface — title and
abstract — not what its full text contains, so constructs reported only in the body
are under-counted. The claim is about the *visibility* of the evidence, which is the
right claim for readers who meet this literature through databases. Where an abstract
is absent the measurement is not made, and the record counts as missing data.

### 2.6 Analysis

Analysis is descriptive throughout. Reporting completeness is reported as n/N with
an explicit N for every construct. The evidence map is a qualitative grid of
mechanism domain against outcome type in which cell counts annotate the narrative
and empty cells are the finding. No effect size is pooled; no confidence interval
is computed; no significance test is performed. Where the corpus supports a
directional statement, the statement is supported by a count of independent studies
and by nothing stronger.

### 2.7 Sensitivity analyses

Five pre-specified analyses test how much of the result depends on a discretionary
choice; all run on artefacts already produced and none retrieves new records.
Results are in Supplementary Table S5.

**E1** replays the automatic-exclusion rule across confidence thresholds. **E2**
compares the conjunctive and disjunctive readings of the group criterion. **E3**
reports each source's exclusive contribution. **E4** reports what the year and
language limits removed. **E5** compares the 7B and 14B layers on the same records,
using Cohen's kappa to describe how far agreement exceeds chance and not to decide
which model is right.

### 2.8 Abstract layer versus full text

Because the whole measurement sits at the abstract, the review tested what the
abstract leaves out. For every included record whose full text could be obtained,
the same twelve detectors were run three times — on the title and abstract, on a
window of the body matched to the abstract's length, and on the whole text. The
paired design means the comparison is within a record, so it cannot be confounded by
differences between papers; the matched window is the control that distinguishes a
document that *states* a construct from a document that merely *contains* the words
for it.

Full text was obtainable for 42 of 170
measurable records (24.7%), a figure that is itself a
finding about the accessibility of this literature: the rest sit behind paywalls that
neither Unpaywall nor CORE could reach. Two checks on the subset: its abstract-level
profile drifts from the corpus by no more than
8.3
percentage points, and the matched window reports *no more* than the abstract for
nine of the twelve constructs, so the lift reported below is not a length effect.

### 2.9 Guiding questions

Three questions were fixed before searching, with the evidence needed for a
directional reading and the condition for reading a gap:

- **A-1.** Do involvement intensity or duration relate directionally to criminal
  role or severity? Directional reading requires at least three independent studies
  reporting involvement parameters in comparable form alongside a graded criminal
  outcome. Gap if fewer, or if directions conflict without a boundary-condition
  explanation.
- **A-2.** Does standardised measurement make cross-study comparison possible?
  Directional reading requires studies that name an instrument and report its score
  with the administration context. Gap if instrument use is rare or reporting
  elements are incomplete.
- **A-3.** Is there a statable relation between the quantification of manipulation
  and judicial outcomes? Directional reading requires documentable pairs of
  manipulation assessment and subsequent legal outcome. Gap if legal outcomes and
  psychological assessments are almost never reported together.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 The design space of mechanisms

The manipulation literature supplies more mechanism labels than it supplies
measurements, and the difference is the first thing this review can quantify.

Coding proceeded from a pre-specified frame rather than from categories discovered
during reading, so that the count answers a fixed question. The BITE frame
distinguishes four domains of control — behaviour, information, thought and emotion
[10]. Each record was coded for the domains it names or squarely
describes, and for whether it names a mechanism term at all.

25 of 170 records name a manipulation mechanism explicitly;
2 name a standardised instrument of any kind. That ratio is the
design space in miniature. The vocabulary of influence is widely shared; the
apparatus for measuring it is nearly absent. A record can therefore assert that
thought reform occurred and cannot state how much, relative to what, or measured
with which instrument. The consequence is not a defect of any single paper but the
equilibrium of a field organised around cases.

In this corpus the vocabulary of manipulation is present but thin: the records that
name a mechanism most often do so through a single term, and the studies that
describe technique in detail are case-based and rarely comparative [11,12].
Where a group's ideology is treated as a mechanism, the analysis is
usually produced by outside observers reconstructing the group's culture, and not through the study's own measurement [13,14].

The four domains are not equally visible. Of the 61
records coded to at least one domain, 5 were
coded to behavioural control, 38 to
information control, 18 to thought control and
14 to emotional control. The ordering matters for
forensic use. Behavioural restriction — movement, contact, dress, work — leaves
documentary traces that case records can cite, so it is the domain most easily
evidenced. The legal argument, however, usually turns on the informational and
emotional domains, where the person's own judgement is displaced, and those are the
domains a court can least easily receive.

A second asymmetry concerns what a mechanism is attached to. A record that names a
mechanism most often attaches it to a group-level process — recruitment, retention,
defection — and describes the criminal act separately, as something a member
happened to do. The mechanism and the offence are then adjacent in the paper and
disjoint in the analysis. Section 3.3 measures how often they are reported together.

### 3.2 The forensic toolchain and its misalignment

Forensic assessment of a person who acted inside a high-control group is, in
practice, an assessment of capacity under constraint. The instruments that exist
for that task were built for neighbouring questions, and the distance between the
question and the instrument is measurable in this corpus.

2 of 170 records name a standardised instrument at all
(Figure 2). That number is the central fact of this section. A field that argues about
responsibility and reports no measurement of the mechanism that is said to
displace it cannot, as a field, put its argument in front of a court in a form the
court can test. 35 records report a legal outcome of any kind.

Where instruments appear, they tend to be instruments for adjacent constructs.
Risk-assessment frameworks estimate the probability of future violence; they do
not measure the degree to which a person's judgement was displaced by a group.
Psychiatric instruments measure delusion and dissociation; a person acting under
coercion is usually not deluded, and the diagnostic threshold is the wrong
threshold for the legal question. The Group Psychological Abuse scale, built for
this domain, asks a member-report question — the varieties of abuse experienced in
the group — which is a good measure of exposure and not a measure of capacity.

Forensic instruments in the corpus measure adjacent questions in exactly this way:
classification schemes built for homicide and suicide typologies, and psychiatric
formulations applied to individual defendants, neither of which takes the group as
the unit of analysis [15,16].

The consequence is a systematic misalignment that has a precise shape: the
assessment tools available ask *what happened to the person* and *what the person
might do*, while the legal question asks *what the person was able to do*. The
middle term is missing, and it is missing for a reason that the corpus makes
visible — measuring it would require an instrument that treats the group, not the
individual, as the unit of analysis, and the literature has almost none.

Two practical consequences follow for the forensic reader. First, an expert asked
to assess a member's responsibility has to construct the mechanism evidence ad hoc,
case by case, which is exactly the condition in which assessments diverge between
experts and between jurisdictions. Second, because no common instrument exists, no
cumulative evidence base can form: each assessment is an argument, and arguments do not aggregate.

### 3.3 Mechanism–crime relations

The three guiding questions were fixed before searching. Their answers here are
counts, not tests.

**A-1: do involvement intensity and duration relate to criminal role or
severity?** 4 of 170 records
distinguish participant roles and report a duration. The pre-specified bar for a
directional reading was three independent studies reporting involvement parameters
in comparable form alongside a graded outcome; that bar is not met in this corpus.
The reading is therefore a gap with a specific shape: role and duration are the two
variables any dose-response claim would need, and the two a record is least likely to
report.

**A-2: does standardised measurement make comparison possible?** The bar was a
record that names an instrument and reports its score with the administration
context. 2 of 170 records name an instrument at all, so the second
clause is not reached and the gap is the near-absence of measurement.

**A-3: is there a statable relation between quantified manipulation and judicial
outcomes?** 5 of 170 records
report both a named mechanism and a legal outcome. The pre-specified bar was
documentable pairs of manipulation assessment and subsequent legal outcome; it is
not met. Records do discuss both in one paper — a case is narrated and the court's reasoning
described — but not as a measured pair, so a reader cannot recover what was assessed
against what was decided.

The records that carry the argument furthest are those where a criminal proceeding
and a group are documented together: prosecutions brought against a group, not an individual member [17,18], cases in which the group's
doctrine enters the court's reasoning [19], and incidents where the mechanism and
the offence are physically inseparable, as in a chemical release [20].

The three questions return one structure: the literature can say that manipulation
was present and what the court decided, and cannot say how much was present or how the
decision related to it. That comparative failure motivates the reporting set in
Section 4.1, and its kind matters. It is not a failure of
scholarship: the individual studies are often careful, and the case literature is
detailed. It is a failure of commensurability — the vocabulary is shared and the
measures are not, so each study speaks to its own case and the accumulation that a
legal argument needs never occurs.

Two further observations belong here. First,
where a record does quantify something, what it quantifies is usually the crime
(how many killed, how much money) rather than the influence; the crime is easier to
count and, being the thing the case is about, is what the record draws from the
court file. Second, the mechanism appears in the corpus almost always as an
interpretive claim attached after the fact — the group's doctrine is offered as the
explanation of the act — and interpretive claims of that form cannot be falsified by
the study that makes them. This is why the recommendation is a reporting set rather
than a research agenda: what is missing is not a new finding but the denominators
that would let existing findings be compared.

### 3.4 The evidence map and the reporting gap

Figure 1 places each included record in a grid of manipulation domain against
criminal outcome type. The grid is sparse, and its sparsity is the result rather
than a shortcoming of the search.

Of the 61 records that could be assigned to a manipulation
domain, the counts by domain are behavioural 5,
informational 38, thought
18 and emotional 14;
a record can carry more than one. Most cells are empty. The densest is
informational control against homicide, which is what a case-record literature would
suggest: the cases that get written up are the ones with a body, and the mechanism
that gets described is the one the court files contain — who was told what, who was
deceived, who was watched.

The reporting profile in Figure 3 is the more consequential result. Offence type
is reported in 83 of 170 records; jurisdiction in
89; data source in 55; legal outcome in
35. The variables that carry the comparative claim are reported far
less often: participant role in 25, involvement or exit duration in
7, sample size in 12, a named mechanism in
25, a standardised instrument in 2, a comparison
or an explicit statement that none was used in 6, and a group
operational definition in 10.

Read as lower bounds, as the validation in Section 2.5 requires. The detector
recall differs by construct — highest for a named mechanism and jurisdiction, lowest
for a group definition and participant role — so the gap between the top and the
bottom of this list is larger than the bars suggest. What the bars
establish is the magnitude of the difference between the two groups: even under the
most generous correction the least reported constructs do not exceed single figures,
while offence type and jurisdiction sit near the ceiling of what an abstract can
carry.

Individual records show why the cells differ in density. A study of a gang-style
cult in Lagos reports victimisation in detail and no instrument [21]; a study of a
single case reports the mechanism as biography and no denominator [22]. Both are
legible, and neither can be set beside the other.

The ordering is not random and it is not a complaint about authors. It follows the
evidentiary base each record draws on. A study built from a court file can report
the offence, the jurisdiction and the outcome, because the file contains them; it
cannot report a duration or a scale score, because the file does not. A study built
from interviews with former members can report a mechanism and a duration and
cannot report a legal outcome, because the interviews did not follow the case to its
end. The literature is composed of records that each see one half of the relation,
and no design in the corpus sees both halves in the same measurement. That is the
structural reason the field displays mechanisms and cannot compare them.

Small and uneven, the corpus costs something, and the review is candid about it.
Records cluster in the most recent decade, come from a relatively small number of
journals, and are dominated by case reports and commentary and not designs with denominators. A scoping review is the right instrument for a literature in this
condition — the point is to map it, not to summarise an effect — but the map is a map
of a thin and heterogeneous body of work, and the reporting set in the next section
should be read as a description of what would make the *next* literature comparable.

### 3.4.1 Where the numbers actually come from

That profile is a property of abstracts, and the review can show it directly.
On the 42 records whose full text was obtained, the same
detectors applied to the full text report substantially more of everything that
matters for comparison: a standardised instrument rises from
0% to
28.6%, a comparison or an
explicit statement that none was used from
2.4% to
35.7%, participant role from
9.5% to
52.4%, legal outcome from
16.7% to
66.7%, and sample size from
7.1% to
45.2% (Figure 5).

The control that makes this interpretable is the middle reading. A window drawn from
the body and cut to the abstract's own length reports no more than the abstract for
nine of the twelve constructs — the abstract is dense and a random stretch of prose
is not — so the difference between the two outer readings is not a difference in word
count. It means the information is *in the paper* and *not in the abstract*. A reader
who meets this literature through a database search, which is how a clinician, a
court clerk or a systematic reviewer meets it, does not see the instrument, the
comparison or the legal outcome even when the study reports them.

This changes what the review is entitled to say, and the change is in the paper's
favour. The literature does not fail to measure mechanism exposure, and it does not
fail to record what the court decided; it fails to surface either at the interface
through which it is read. The field's comparability problem is therefore a reporting
problem, which is solvable by an editorial convention, rather than a methodological
problem, which would need new instruments and new studies.

Two limits on that conclusion. Only a quarter of the included records yielded full
text, and the quarter that did is biased towards open-access and health-sciences
publishing; the lift measured here may differ in a corpus of closed-access law
journals. And a full text mentioning an instrument is not the same as a study using
it well — the measurement shows that the information is available to a reader of the
paper, not that the underlying study is sound.

### 3.5 Investigative and regulatory practice in the corpus

The research topic asks about forensic implications, and behind them sits a question
about what a state can actually do. Two layers of practice lie behind the reporting
profile, and the corpus lets us count how visible each one is.

This section opens with the investigative modules M8–M10 that
Section 3.4 introduced; the second half reports the regulatory layer and reads both
in governance terms.

**The investigative layer.** Of 170 measurable records,

These are gap findings, not judgements of capability. Investigative practice exists in

absent public trace, not an absent practice. This is Section 3.4.1 seen from the other
side: there the information was in the paper and not in the abstract, here the practice
is in the file and not in the paper.

**The regulatory layer.** Regulatory instruments are named even less often. Of
170 records, 19
(11.2%) name any instrument of this kind, and only
3 name more than one. Criminal prosecution
dominates the small set that is named
(14, 8.2%),

civil suits against new religious movements [17], the application of trafficking law to
high-control groups together with proposed amendments [18], and sentencing principles in
cult-related cases [19].

That distribution is a governance finding. MRS-CI is best understood against it: an
information-disclosure-based, low-burden regulatory instrument. It imposes no licence and
no sanction; it changes what must be made visible, so that otherwise incomparable case
narratives become an auditable and comparable record.

The asymmetry is the finding. Every instrument these records name acts on conduct after
the event: prosecution, removal or status, trafficking enforcement, family-court
remedies. No record names a registration, reporting or supervisory requirement imposed
before harm, and the two categories that would capture such a requirement —
charitable-status regulation and civil commitment — return nothing. Two records go
further and propose amending enforcement statutes. Neither proposes an instrument that
operates on information.

That shape follows from what this class of harm looks like. Incidents are rare, and
severe when they occur; the corpus is a record of those incidents. Enforcement arrives
after the damage and cannot be calibrated to it. The field's regulatory imagination is
anchored to the cases that produced it.

Read in those terms, MRS-CI belongs to a recognisable sequence of regulatory instruments.
It first makes risk countable: items 1 and 6 to 9 turn a group, an offence and a legal
outcome into quantities a regulator can tally. It then supplies the parameters an
assessment needs: items 2, 3 and 5 give exposure and capacity, who the person was, how
long, measured how. Identification, assessment, disclosure: that sequence is the
ordinary shape of risk governance, and the same shape appears in risk-assessment
registration, in algorithmic accountability regimes and in the regulatory analysis of
irrational exuberance in the AI industry [26].

The parallel is structural, not empirical. The review shows that the disclosure layer is
missing from this literature. It offers no evidence that adding the layer would reduce
harm, and a corpus of a few hundred summaries cannot supply that evidence. What can be
said is narrower. A regime that requires nothing but a comparable sentence is cheap
enough to try, and comparable enough to be evaluated.

One count points the same way. Online recruitment appears in
5 of 170 records, and
the study of internet manipulation by sect members [12] is one of the few that treats
the channel as an object of analysis; most records treat it as a setting. Platforms are
the natural site of an ex-ante disclosure duty, and the corpus is close to silent about
them.

Read against the topic's three themes: manipulation technique is what items 4 and 5
make countable, group dynamics is what items 2 and 3 make comparable, and forensic
implication is what items 6 to 9 place in a form a court can weigh. The investigative
counts show which of those the literature currently makes available to a fact-finder,
and the regulatory counts show which it makes available to a regulator — in both cases,
the answer for this corpus is: fewer than the underlying cases contain.

This subsection codes only what published literature states about practice. It is neither an investigative guide nor a legislative proposal, and it draws on no unpublished or
restricted case material.

## 4. Discussion

This section addresses three audiences at once: reviewers, for what to require of this
literature; investigators, for what the record does and does not support; and
regulators, for where the instrument gap sits.

### 4.1 A minimum reporting set (MRS-CI)

The minimum reporting set (Table 2) is derived from what the corpus reports, and not from committee preference — and Section 3.4.1 fixes its target. The information the set asks for
already exists in the papers; what fails is its transit to the abstract. A set
addressed at that transit is unusually cheap: it does not ask any author to measure
anything new, and it does not ask any journal to change its peer review. It asks for
one sentence per item in the place a reader actually looks. Each item below exists
because its measured frequency is low, and each is stated in the minimum form that
would make the next study comparable, measured as described in Section 2.5 over
170 records.

**Table 2.** Minimum reporting set (MRS-CI): the ten items, the minimum acceptable
form of each, and the frequency with which the corpus reports it (n/170 records
with an abstract).

| # | Item | Minimum acceptable form | Measured | Why it is necessary |
|:---|:-----------|:------------------------|:-----------|:--------------------------------|
| 1 | Group operational definition | the criterion used to classify the group, and its source, in one sentence | 10/170 (5.9%) | "cult" means different things in different jurisdictions; without a definition the inclusion boundary cannot be audited |
| 2 | Participant role | leader / core executor / ordinary member / victim / former member, with the basis for the classification | 25/170 (14.7%) | "cult member" collapses the different levels of agency that the legal question is about |
| 3 | Involvement and exit duration | month-level start and end, or years; an explicit "unknown" is acceptable | 7/170 (4.1%) | duration is the closest available proxy for dose, and dose is what a mechanism claim needs |
| 4 | Named manipulation mechanism | the term actually used, from a checkable list | 25/170 (14.7%) | mechanism statements are currently rhetorical enumerations; without a term list they cannot be counted across studies |
| 5 | Standardised instrument | name, score, and administration context (who administered, when, in what setting) | 2/170 (1.2%) | a score without its method is not comparable; a negative statement ("no instrument used") also satisfies the item |
| 6 | Offence type | offence category under a named classification (statutory or study-internal) | 83/170 (48.8%) | "involved in crime" cannot carry a comparison across jurisdictions |
| 7 | Offence severity | a statutory grade, a study-internal grade, or a sentence length | 4/170 (2.4%) | severity is the outcome variable a dose-response claim would use |
| 8 | Legal outcome | charge, conviction, sentence, responsibility finding or ruling, with the date | 35/170 (20.6%) | this is the interface between the psychological literature and legal practice; absent, the two cannot be joined |
| 9 | Jurisdiction | country and the applicable responsibility framework | 89/170 (52.4%) | responsibility standards differ between jurisdictions; the difference is itself a boundary condition |
| 10 | Data source and recruitment | clinical / forensic / self-report / media / court file, and how participants entered the study | 55/170 (32.4%) | the source type bounds the causal claim that can be made |

Two further requirements belong to general reporting hygiene and not to this domain and are recommended alongside the ten: a comparison group, a baseline, or an
explicit statement that none was used (6/170); and a sample
size for the persons, cases or documents analysed (12/170). Their
measured frequency is among the lowest of anything counted, which is why they are
named here even though they are not specific to cultic influence.

Three properties are deliberate. Items 3, 5 and 10 can be satisfied by an explicit
negative statement — "duration unknown", "no instrument used", "no court file" —
because the failure mode being removed is silence, not the absence of a measurement.
Items 1–3 form a block: a role without a duration does not explain anything. And no
item asks for a new experiment: every one can be satisfied from data a study already
has, which is what makes the set a reporting standard, not a research programme.

Item 4 is the one most likely to be contested by authors, and the minimum form is
therefore the weakest that still permits counting: the term, not a definition of the
term.

Three of the items do double duty. Items 2, 3 and 8 — participant role, involvement
and exit duration, legal outcome — are what make an investigative file comparable
between cases as well as what make a study comparable between papers; the same
sentence, written once, serves the evidentiary record and the regulatory one. This
is the point taken up in Section 3.5.

Section 3.5 reads those three items in regulatory terms as well: a role, a duration and
a legal outcome are what any ex-ante disclosure obligation would have to record to be
auditable at all. One sentence then serves three readers — the reviewer comparing
studies, the investigator comparing files, the regulator comparing groups.

### 4.2 What legal translation requires

A legal system can absorb a psychological finding when four conditions are met.
The corpus supports an assessment of each, with different answers.

*Quantifiability comes first: the mechanism must be expressible in a form the court can weigh.* This is not
met. 2 records name an instrument, and the instruments that exist
measure exposure rather than capacity. An expert can describe a mechanism; the
court has no scale against which to compare the description with the same
description in another case.

*Reviewability is the second condition: the assessment must be recorded in a form that can be reviewed.* The legal
question is about the person's state at the time of the act; forensic practice
records the person's state at the time of assessment. Where records do report a
legal outcome (35/170), the assessment that preceded it is rarely
recoverable from the same text, so the reasoning cannot be audited even in
principle.

*Third, the relation must be expressible as evidence, not as narrative.* A case
narrative can persuade; it cannot be cross-examined on its denominators. This is
where the reporting set does its legal work: items 2 and 3 (role, duration) and
item 8 (legal outcome) are precisely the elements that would let a court see
whether a mechanism claim is being applied consistently across cases in the same
jurisdiction.

*Finally, the intervention consequence must be specifiable.* If a person's capacity was
constrained by a group, the legal and clinical consequences differ from those that
follow from an individual disposition — different risk management, different
conditions, different prospects on leaving. The corpus reports intervention
outcomes in a minority of records and almost never links them to a legal outcome.

None of these conditions requires a change in law. Each requires a change in what
studies put in their abstracts, which is the cheapest possible intervention and the
only one available to a review.

### 4.3 Outlook: three questions worth testing

Three questions follow from the map. Each is stated in a form that a future
literature could settle, and each is left without a formal falsification design
because that design is an empirical study.

**Q1. Does role-graded reporting change the consistency of mechanism–crime
findings?** If records began to classify participants by role and duration, the
same mechanism should be associated with different outcomes at different levels,
and the association should look more consistent across studies than it does now.
The prediction is testable with the same corpus re-read in five years: the
between-study variance in what a mechanism is said to cause should fall when role
is held constant.

**Q2. Does adoption of a reporting set precede or follow empirical progress?** If
the set is adopted, it should appear first in the studies that already have data —
it is cheap to report what one has measured — and only later change what is
measured. The prediction is that adoption of MRS-CI will be visible before any
change in the underlying instruments, and that instrument development will follow adoption.

**Q3. Is the legal outcome the binding constraint?** If the interface is where the
literature fails — mechanism reported, outcome not — then studies that do report a
legal outcome should be disproportionately those built from court files, and
studies built from interviews should almost never follow a case to judgment. The
prediction is a strong association between data-source type and whether a legal
outcome is reported, which the current corpus suggests but is too small to
establish.

The three questions share a property: they can be answered by counting, not
by arguing. That is the same property the review itself relied on, and it is the
reason the recommendation is a reporting set rather than a theory. A theory of
cultic influence on crime is not what this literature lacks; it has several. What
it lacks is the ability to tell which of them the data support.

## 5. Conclusion

Cultic influence on crime, as a literature, measures more than its abstracts say.
Offence type appears in 83 of 170 abstracts and a standardised
instrument in 2; on the subset whose full text could be read, the
instrument figure rises from 0%
to 28.6% and the legal-outcome
figure from 16.7% to
66.7%. The variable that makes
comparison possible is not withheld from the scholarship; it is withheld from the
reading interface.

That pattern is not an indictment of the scholars who produced this literature, and
Section 3.4.1 says why: the papers contain what their abstracts omit. What a field
built on the case record does inherit is the shape of the case in its *summary* — the
court saw the offence and the doctrine, so the abstract leads with the offence and
the doctrine, and the instrument and the comparison drift to the methods section
where no reader of a database reaches them.

The consequence for forensic practice is immediate. An expert asked whether a
defendant's capacity was constrained by a group has to build the argument from
scratch in every case, and no two experts are guaranteed to build it the same way.
The consequence for research follows from where the gap sits. Because the
comparable quantities do not reach the abstract, a reader assembling a literature
cannot accumulate one, and the field remains a collection of well-told cases even
where the underlying studies are careful. The remedy is correspondingly modest, and
that is the point: the review is not asking for a research programme but for four or
five sentences that the data already support.

A reporting set this small asks for no new data. Its purpose is to convert a literature that can describe into a literature
that can be compared. Whether that happens is an empirical question, and answering
it is a matter of counting.
