## Abstract

**Background.** High-control groups sit at the junction of social psychology, group dynamics and criminal conduct. Mental manipulation, coercive control and indoctrination shape how courts and clinicians attribute agency, and therefore findings of criminal responsibility. The relation between these mechanisms and criminal outcomes is widely discussed and rarely compared. **Objective.** To map what the literature on cultic influence and crime demonstrates, what it measures and what it leaves unreported, and to derive a minimum reporting set from that map. **Methods.** Scoping review following PRISMA-ScR and JBI guidance, specified before searching. Six bibliographic sources were searched from 1994 to November 2026 with a base block requiring a high-control-group term and a crime term in title or abstract. Records were screened against four criteria by a fixed open-weight language model, with every verdict, supporting span and confidence retained; reporting completeness was then measured by twelve documented lexical detectors, each returning its matched span so that every count can be re-derived. No effect size was pooled and no significance test was computed. **Results.** {{N_INCLUDED}} records met the inclusion criteria, {{N_INCLUDED_MEASURABLE}} of which carried an abstract; full text was obtainable for {{fulltext_lift.n_pairs}} of them ({{fulltext_lift.availability_pct}}%). Among the measurable abstracts, {{PCT_GROUP_DEF}}% stated how the group was operationally identified, {{PCT_ROLE}}% distinguished participant roles, {{PCT_DURATION}}% reported involvement or exit duration, {{PCT_INSTRUMENT}}% named a standardised instrument and {{PCT_LEGAL}}% reported a legal outcome. The evidence map is sparse. Most mechanism–outcome cells are empty. **Conclusions.** The variables that carry the causal claim — role, duration, instrument, legal outcome — are the least reported in the abstract, but they are not missing from the papers. A paired reading of abstracts against full texts raises the standardised-instrument rate from {{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C5_instrument.abstract_pct}}% to {{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C5_instrument.fulltext_pct}}%, and the legal-outcome rate from {{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C8_legal.abstract_pct}}% to {{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C8_legal.fulltext_pct}}%. The deficit sits at the reading interface rather than in the scholarship, and a ten-item minimum reporting set is proposed for that interface, framed as a disclosure instrument a regulator could impose at low cost.

## 1. Introduction

A cult, in the sense this review uses, is not a belief system but an organisation
that constrains exit. The distinction matters legally: what a court needs to know
is not whether a doctrine is eccentric but whether a person's capacity to choose
was systematically narrowed, by whom, over what period, and with what consequence.
That question sits at the junction of three literatures that do not usually read
each other — the social psychology of influence, the criminology of deviant groups,
and the forensic assessment of responsibility.

Each literature answers a part of it. Social psychology has produced the mechanism
vocabulary: coercive control, thought reform, information control, trauma bonding,
groupthink, obedience. Criminology has produced the outcome vocabulary: offence
types, victim selection, organisational structure, network concealment. Forensic
psychology has produced the instruments: structured professional judgement tools,
risk frameworks, and the general apparatus of criminal responsibility assessment.
What has not been produced, at least not in a form that can be audited, is the
bridge: a body of work in which a named mechanism is measured in a comparable way
and set against a named criminal or judicial outcome.

This absence is not for want of cases. Jonestown, Aum Shinrikyo, the Manson family,
the Order of the Solar Temple and a long tail of smaller groups supply an abundant
case record, and case-based scholarship on them is substantial. The gap is
structural rather than empirical. Case material establishes that a mechanism can
operate; it cannot establish how often, in whom, against which outcome, because
each case supplies its own definitions, its own measures and its own vocabulary.
When a literature is built this way the consequence is a reading problem as much as
an evidentiary one: nothing in a case report obliges it to surface, in a form another
study can use, who the person was in the group, how long they were there, or what the
court decided. The field can display mechanisms; assembling those displays into a
comparable body of work is the step that does not happen.

Prior syntheses exist, and none is a systematic map. The closest is an editorial
handbook for forensic practitioners, which integrates theory, clinical and legal
material in narrative form and states that systematic research in this area is
scarce {durns}. The only review focused on violent outcomes is seventeen years old
and restricted to violence {lalich}. Work on joining and leaving
examines recruitment and exit rather than offending {rousselet}. A systematic review of
coercive control exists, but it is anchored in intimate partner violence rather than
in groups {lohmann}. Two studies established that the cult–crime intersection is
empirically reachable {bohm,coates}. None of them supplies a reproducible corpus, a
measure of reporting completeness, or a reporting standard.

That consequence is the object of this review, and it is measurable. If the
comparison problem is real, it should show up as a specific pattern of reporting:
the variables that make comparison possible — who the person was in the group, how
long they were there, which instrument measured what, what the court decided —
should be the least frequently reported. Variables that carry no comparative work,
such as the name of the group or a narrative of the mechanism, should be reported
almost universally. The pattern is a testable prediction about the corpus, and this
review tests it by counting.

Four contributions follow. First, the reading-interface finding: the quantities that
make studies comparable are in the papers and absent from their abstracts, on a paired
comparison with a length-matched control. Second, a comparative map across twelve
constructs, each reported as n/N with its denominator. Third, a map of the
investigative record and what it does and does not contain (Section 3.5). Fourth, the
regulatory asymmetry: every instrument the corpus names is ex post, none operates on
information, and MRS-CI is proposed as a disclosure-based instrument of low burden.
The binding constraint on this field is the comparability of what studies report.

The review is scoping rather than systematic-with-meta-analysis, and this is a
substantive position. The mechanism variables are measured with different
instruments, in different legal systems, on data of different provenance. Pooling
them would estimate the pooled distribution of measurement conventions, not the
effect of manipulation on crime. PRISMA-ScR and the JBI guidance accommodate
exactly this situation: descriptive statistics and qualitative content analysis,
no pooled estimate, no significance test.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

Its conduct was specified before searching: population, concept and context; the
search strategy; the eligibility criteria; the screening and extraction procedures;
and three guiding questions with their anticipated judgements. The PRISMA-ScR
checklist {tricco} is submitted with the manuscript and the JBI
updated guidance {peters} was followed for conduct and reporting.
Deviations from the protocol, and the constraint that forced each, are listed in
Supplementary Table S1; they are summarised where they bear on interpretation.

### 2.2 Eligibility

**Population.** People involved in high-control groups — leaders, core executors,
ordinary members, victims, and former members — and the empirical studies, case
reports, forensic assessments and court documents built on them. The unit of analysis is the record.

**Concept.** The relation between a psychological manipulation mechanism
(indoctrination, thought reform, coercive control, mind control, social isolation,
trauma bonding, groupthink, cognitive dissonance, information control, obedience)
and a criminal or judicial outcome, together with the completeness with which the
mechanism and the outcome are reported.

**Context.** Published, peer-reviewed, legal and grey literature in English, from
1994 — the year the Group Psychological Abuse scale was published {chambers} and the point from which empirical work in this area becomes continuous —
to the search date.

Four criteria were applied to titles and abstracts: **I1** the record concerns a
cult or high-control group as an actual organised group; **I2** it concerns criminal
conduct or the criminal-justice process; **I3** a manipulation mechanism is
analysed rather than merely named; **I4** it carries empirical or documentary
material. The four are conjunctive. I1 is what excludes the large metaphorical
literature — "cult" films, the "cult of the double-blind trial" — that any keyword
search on this topic retrieves in quantity, and I2 is what implements the target
topic's own boundary, which excludes non-criminal cult activity.

### 2.3 Sources and search

Six sources were searched. Crossref ({{SRC_CROSSREF}} records passing the base
block), Semantic Scholar ({{SRC_S2}}), CORE ({{SRC_CORE}}), Europe PMC
({{SRC_EPMC}} in the primary tier, plus a widened tier), PubMed ({{SRC_PUBMED}})
and DOAJ ({{SRC_DOAJ}}) together retrieved {{N_RETRIEVED}} records before
deduplication.

The choice of sources follows a constraint. OpenAlex, the protocol's original primary source, moved in February 2026 to credit
metering; its free allowance was exhausted during pre-search development and every
request returned HTTP 429 with a retry-after of about eighteen hours. The search was
rebuilt on sources measurable on the execution date, and their yields differ. CORE
returned 3,046 hits for the composite query but its unkeyed quota allowed
{{SRC_CORE}} records before persistent throttling; Crossref ranks by relevance and
yields few on-topic records per thousand rows; Semantic Scholar served {{SRC_S2}}
records only after repeated 429 backoff. The biomedical sources remain the weakest fit
for a criminological and legal literature, which is itself a finding about where this
field publishes.

Grey literature followed the same rule. Three policy documents inform the
background — the Victorian Parliament committee report on harm in high-control groups,
the Family Survival Trust report on coercive control in UK cultic groups, and the
McIvor et al. review — and none entered the corpus: none is peer-reviewed and none
reports a codable mechanism–outcome pair. They are cited as context, not counted.

The search used a base block plus five nested blocks. The base block requires at
least one high-control-group term and at least one crime term in the title or
abstract; the nested blocks add manipulation-mechanism terms, offence-type terms,
measurement terms, forensic and legal terms, and intervention and exit terms, and
are reported as filters with their own counts. The full strategy, per-source counts, retrieval dates and artefact
hashes are in Supplementary Table S2.

### 2.4 Screening

Records were screened against I1–I4 by a fixed open-weight language model running
locally on one RTX 4090, one record per prompt, greedy decoding, fixed seed. The
production screen used Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct at 4-bit AWQ; a 7B model
(Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) screened the same records for the model-agreement experiment
E5. For each criterion the model returned a verdict together with a verbatim span
from the record, and only then a decision and a confidence; the order is part of the
method, because in the pilot the model answered "everything is irrelevant" when the
decision field came first and located evidence correctly when the span came first.

Three gates convert the model's output into an inclusion decision, and each is computed from the output. First, the decision is
computed from the four criterion flags, not read from the model's own decision
field: the pilot caught the model returning INCLUDE while one of its own criteria
was false. Second, the group criterion is conjunctive — a record must carry a
lexical group anchor (cult, sect, high-control group, new religious movement,
destructive cult, charismatic group, commune) *and* receive a positive I1 verdict.
The lexical half exists because the model treated intimate partner violence,
commercial sexual exploitation and a nerve-agent attack as cult contexts; the
semantic half exists because a title can contain the word without the topic.
Third, automatic exclusion requires EXCLUDE with confidence of at least 0.90, and is
overridden for any record whose own title carries both a group term and a crime
term.

A validation read tested this. Fifty records — twenty-five included and
twenty-five auto-excluded — were read against the four criteria. Of the twenty-five
inclusions, {{validation_numbers.tally.Y}} were clearly on topic, {{validation_numbers.tally.M}} were
arguable and {{validation_numbers.tally.N}} were not: the errors are
records where "cult" is a metaphor (veneration, fashion, film criticism) or where a
criminal organisation is not a high-control group. Of the twenty-five auto-exclusions,
23 were correctly excluded and 2 were arguably retrievable, both legal analyses
sitting at the cult/crime boundary. Two consequences follow, and both are carried into
the reading of the results. First, the {{validation_numbers.nonqualifying_matched}} non-qualifying records are not inert
denominator: they report a mean of {{validation_numbers.nonqualifying_mean_constructs}} constructs
against a corpus mean of {{validation_numbers.corpus_mean_constructs}}, and
{{validation_numbers.nonqualifying_jurisdiction_n}} of them report a jurisdiction, which is
one of the two largest bars in Figure 3. Removing them would move offence type from
{{validation_numbers.offence_corpus_pct}}% to {{validation_numbers.offence_without_pct}}% and
jurisdiction from {{validation_numbers.jurisdiction_corpus_pct}}% to
{{validation_numbers.jurisdiction_without_pct}}% — in both directions, and by about a
percentage point. The
profile therefore does not depend on the screen's precision, but the direction of the
error is not the one that would flatter the paper: the corpus is not biased towards
over-reporting, and it is not biased towards under-reporting either. Second, no
precision claim is made for the screen; the sample is small, the reader is the executing agent, not an independent reviewer, and the operative check will be the
author adjudication of the full table, which has not yet been performed.
Supplementary Table S7 gives the verdicts record by record.

Two disclosures belong here. No double
independent screening by two human reviewers was performed; what replaces the
inter-rater statistic is the full decision trail, a prompt-perturbation re-run that
measures how far decisions move when the criterion wording is paraphrased, and a
count of how many auto-excluded records a more permissive reading would have
rescued. These are stability and sensitivity, and none of them is an estimate of
human agreement. The second pass was rule-based rather than human: adjudication by
the named authors remains outstanding, and the numbers here are the machine record,
not a claim of human review. In the pilot the model's residual errors were
concentrated in one direction — it over-admitted intimate-partner-violence records
before the I1 gate was made explicit, and over-rejected when asked to judge twelve
constructs at once — and both are recorded in the repository's deviation log. The
validation sample will be read blind by a second author, and the two readings will be
reported side by side in Supplementary Table S7, completed together with the author
adjudication in Table S3.

### 2.5 Measurement of reporting completeness

Reporting completeness was measured at the level of title and abstract, over twelve
constructs: the ten items of the minimum reporting set (Table 2), plus sample size
and comparison. Each construct was measured by a
documented detector — a term list, a numeric pattern or a two-condition rule, with
the full set given in the repository and summarised in Supplementary Table S2. A
construct counts as *reported* when its detector fires, and the detector returns the
matched span, which is reproduced in the extraction table for every record so that
any reader can inspect the basis of any flag and can disagree with the term list and
recount.

The detectors were themselves validated, because everything in Section 3.4 rests on
them. Thirty records drawn at random were coded by hand against the same twelve
constructs, blind to the detector output, and the two readings compared. The
detectors are precise and conservative: overall precision 0.80, overall recall 0.45,
with recall uneven across constructs — 0.62 for a named mechanism, 0.60 for
jurisdiction, 0.52 for offence type, 0.45 for legal outcome, but only 0.24 for a group
operational definition and 0.25 for participant role. It means
the reported percentages are lower bounds, and that the *distance* between a
high-recall and a low-recall construct is partly a measurement effect rather than a
property of the literature. Where the argument depends on the ordering, the paper
therefore leans on the comparison that survives the correction: the two least
reported constructs are named in {{PCT_C5_instrument}}% and {{PCT_C7_severity}}% of records
(standardised instrument, Table 2; offence severity), and even if their recall matched the best-observed
construct (0.62) they would rise only to about {{recall_corrected.C5_instrument}}% and {{recall_corrected.C7_severity}}% — still an order of
magnitude below offence type and jurisdiction. The same
read gives precision by construct between 0.73 and 1.00 where it can be estimated,
so the error runs in the direction of under-counting, not over-counting.

A language-model extractor was implemented first and abandoned: on a pilot of 28
records it marked all twelve constructs absent for abstracts that plainly named an
offence type and a jurisdiction, and its verbatim anchors failed a string re-check in
30% of the cases where it did fire. A lexical detector measures *whether the words are
there*, and that is exactly the claim the reporting set is about.

The obvious alternative was tested. The same 29 hand-coded records were put to the
14B model one construct at a time — the prompt form that works for it — and scored
against the hand read: precision {{llm_read_scores.precision}}, recall
{{llm_read_scores.recall}}, rising to 0.62 only for a named mechanism. It would have
reported offence type in 2 of 29 records where the hand read finds it in all 29. An
instrument right when it fires and silent most of the time cannot carry a prevalence
estimate, which is why the measurement layer is lexical.

What is measured is what a record states at its reading interface — title and
abstract — not what its full text contains, so constructs reported only in the body
are under-counted. The claim is about the *visibility* of the evidence, which is the
right claim for readers who meet this literature through databases. Where an abstract
is absent the measurement is not made, and the record counts as missing data.

### 2.6 Analysis

Analysis is descriptive throughout. Reporting completeness is reported as n/N with
an explicit N for every construct. The evidence map is a qualitative grid of
mechanism domain against outcome type in which cell counts annotate the narrative
and empty cells are the finding. No effect size is pooled; no confidence interval
is computed; no significance test is performed. Where the corpus supports a
directional statement, the statement is supported by a count of independent studies
and by nothing stronger.

### 2.7 Sensitivity analyses

Five pre-specified analyses test how much of the result depends on a discretionary
choice; all run on artefacts already produced and none retrieves new records.
Results are in Supplementary Table S5.

**E1** replays the automatic-exclusion rule across confidence thresholds. **E2**
compares the conjunctive and disjunctive readings of the group criterion. **E3**
reports each source's exclusive contribution. **E4** reports what the year and
language limits removed. **E5** compares the 7B and 14B layers on the same records,
using Cohen's kappa to describe how far agreement exceeds chance and not to decide
which model is right.

### 2.8 Abstract layer versus full text

Because the whole measurement sits at the abstract, the review tested what the
abstract leaves out. For every included record whose full text could be obtained,
the same twelve detectors were run three times — on the title and abstract, on a
window of the body matched to the abstract's length, and on the whole text. The
paired design means the comparison is within a record, so it cannot be confounded by
differences between papers; the matched window is the control that distinguishes a
document that *states* a construct from a document that merely *contains* the words
for it.

Full text was obtainable for {{fulltext_lift.n_pairs}} of {{N_INCLUDED_MEASURABLE}}
measurable records ({{fulltext_lift.availability_pct}}%), a figure that is itself a
finding about the accessibility of this literature: the rest sit behind paywalls that
neither Unpaywall nor CORE could reach. Two checks on the subset: its abstract-level
profile drifts from the corpus by no more than
{{fulltext_lift.max_abs_drift_pp}}
percentage points, and the matched window reports *no more* than the abstract for
nine of the twelve constructs, so the lift reported below is not a length effect.

### 2.9 Guiding questions

Three questions were fixed before searching, with the evidence needed for a
directional reading and the condition for reading a gap:

- **A-1.** Do involvement intensity or duration relate directionally to criminal
  role or severity? Directional reading requires at least three independent studies
  reporting involvement parameters in comparable form alongside a graded criminal
  outcome. Gap if fewer, or if directions conflict without a boundary-condition
  explanation.
- **A-2.** Does standardised measurement make cross-study comparison possible?
  Directional reading requires studies that name an instrument and report its score
  with the administration context. Gap if instrument use is rare or reporting
  elements are incomplete.
- **A-3.** Is there a statable relation between the quantification of manipulation
  and judicial outcomes? Directional reading requires documentable pairs of
  manipulation assessment and subsequent legal outcome. Gap if legal outcomes and
  psychological assessments are almost never reported together.
