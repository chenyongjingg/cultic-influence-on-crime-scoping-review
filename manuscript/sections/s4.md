Forensic assessment of a person who acted inside a high-control group is, in
practice, an assessment of capacity under constraint. The instruments that exist
for that task were built for neighbouring questions, and the distance between the
question and the instrument is measurable in this corpus.

{{N_C5_instrument}} of {{N_ALL}} records name a standardised instrument at all
(Figure 2). That number is the central fact of this section. A field that argues about
responsibility and reports no measurement of the mechanism that is said to
displace it cannot, as a field, put its argument in front of a court in a form the
court can test. {{N_C8_legal}} records report a legal outcome of any kind.

Where instruments appear, they tend to be instruments for adjacent constructs.
Risk-assessment frameworks estimate the probability of future violence; they do
not measure the degree to which a person's judgement was displaced by a group.
Psychiatric instruments measure delusion and dissociation; a person acting under
coercion is usually not deluded, and the diagnostic threshold is the wrong
threshold for the legal question. The Group Psychological Abuse scale, built for
this domain, asks a member-report question — the varieties of abuse experienced in
the group — which is a good measure of exposure and not a measure of capacity.

Forensic instruments in the corpus measure adjacent questions in exactly this way:
classification schemes built for homicide and suicide typologies, and psychiatric
formulations applied to individual defendants, neither of which takes the group as
the unit of analysis {felthous,akimoto1997}.

The consequence is a systematic misalignment that has a precise shape: the
assessment tools available ask *what happened to the person* and *what the person
might do*, while the legal question asks *what the person was able to do*. The
middle term is missing, and it is missing for a reason that the corpus makes
visible — measuring it would require an instrument that treats the group, not the
individual, as the unit of analysis, and the literature has almost none.

Two practical consequences follow for the forensic reader. First, an expert asked
to assess a member's responsibility has to construct the mechanism evidence ad hoc,
case by case, which is exactly the condition in which assessments diverge between
experts and between jurisdictions. Second, because no common instrument exists, no
cumulative evidence base can form: each assessment is an argument, not a
measurement, and arguments do not aggregate.
