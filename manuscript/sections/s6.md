Figure 1 places each included record in a grid of manipulation domain against
criminal outcome type. The grid is sparse, and its sparsity is the result rather
than a shortcoming of the search.

Of the {{domain_counts.any_domain}} records that could be assigned to a manipulation
domain, the counts by domain are behavioural {{domain_counts.per_domain.behaviour}},
informational {{domain_counts.per_domain.information}}, thought
{{domain_counts.per_domain.thought}} and emotional {{domain_counts.per_domain.emotion}};
a record can carry more than one. Most cells are empty. The densest is
informational control against homicide, which is what a case-record literature would
suggest: the cases that get written up are the ones with a body, and the mechanism
that gets described is the one the court files contain — who was told what, who was
deceived, who was watched.

The reporting profile in Figure 3 is the more consequential result. Offence type
is reported in {{N_C6_offence}} of {{N_ALL}} records; jurisdiction in
{{N_C9_jurisdiction}}; data source in {{N_C10_source}}; legal outcome in
{{N_C8_legal}}. The variables that carry the comparative claim are reported far
less often: participant role in {{N_C2_role}}, involvement or exit duration in
{{N_C3_duration}}, sample size in {{N_C12_n}}, a named mechanism in
{{N_C4_mechanism}}, a standardised instrument in {{N_C5_instrument}}, a comparison
or an explicit statement that none was used in {{N_C11_comparison}}, and a group
operational definition in {{N_C1_group_def}}.

Read as lower bounds, as the validation in Section 2.5 requires. The detector
recall differs by construct — highest for a named mechanism and jurisdiction, lowest
for a group definition and participant role — so the gap between the top and the
bottom of this list is larger than the bars suggest, not smaller. What the bars
establish is the magnitude of the difference between the two groups: even under the
most generous correction the least reported constructs do not exceed single figures,
while offence type and jurisdiction sit near the ceiling of what an abstract can
carry.

The ordering is not random and it is not a complaint about authors. It follows the
evidentiary base each record draws on. A study built from a court file can report
the offence, the jurisdiction and the outcome, because the file contains them; it
cannot report a duration or a scale score, because the file does not. A study built
from interviews with former members can report a mechanism and a duration and
cannot report a legal outcome, because the interviews did not follow the case to its
end. The literature is composed of records that each see one half of the relation,
and no design in the corpus sees both halves in the same measurement. That is the
structural reason the field displays mechanisms and cannot compare them.

The corpus is small and uneven, and the review is candid about what that costs.
Records cluster in the most recent decade, come from a relatively small number of
journals, and are dominated by case reports and commentary rather than by designs
with denominators. A scoping review is the right instrument for a literature in this
condition — the point is to map it, not to summarise an effect — but the map is a map
of a thin and heterogeneous body of work, and the reporting set in the next section
should be read as a description of what would make the *next* literature comparable
rather than as a criticism of this one.

### 6.1 Where the numbers actually come from

The profile above is a property of abstracts, and the review can show that directly.
On the {{fulltext_lift.n_pairs}} records whose full text was obtained, the same
detectors applied to the full text report substantially more of everything that
matters for comparison: a standardised instrument rises from
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C5_instrument.abstract_pct}}% to
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C5_instrument.fulltext_pct}}%, a comparison or an
explicit statement that none was used from
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C11_comparison.abstract_pct}}% to
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C11_comparison.fulltext_pct}}%, participant role from
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C2_role.abstract_pct}}% to
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C2_role.fulltext_pct}}%, legal outcome from
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C8_legal.abstract_pct}}% to
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C8_legal.fulltext_pct}}%, and sample size from
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C12_n.abstract_pct}}% to
{{fulltext_lift.per_construct.C12_n.fulltext_pct}}% (Figure 5).

The control that makes this interpretable is the middle reading. A window drawn from
the body and cut to the abstract's own length reports no more than the abstract for
nine of the twelve constructs — the abstract is dense and a random stretch of prose
is not — so the difference between the two outer readings is not a difference in word
count. It means the information is *in the paper* and *not in the abstract*. A reader
who meets this literature through a database search, which is how a clinician, a
court clerk or a systematic reviewer meets it, does not see the instrument, the
comparison or the legal outcome even when the study reports them.

This changes what the review is entitled to say, and the change is in the paper's
favour. The literature does not fail to measure mechanism exposure, and it does not
fail to record what the court decided; it fails to surface either at the interface
through which it is read. The field's comparability problem is therefore a reporting
problem, which is solvable by an editorial convention, rather than a methodological
problem, which would need new instruments and new studies.

Two limits on that conclusion. Only a quarter of the included records yielded full
text, and the quarter that did is biased towards open-access and health-sciences
publishing; the lift measured here may differ in a corpus of closed-access law
journals. And a full text mentioning an instrument is not the same as a study using
it well — the measurement shows that the information is available to a reader of the
paper, not that the underlying study is sound.

### 6.2 The investigative dimension

The research topic asks about forensic implications, so the corpus was also coded
for three investigative elements that a case-based literature might be expected to
carry: interrogation and confession practice (M8), digital and financial evidence
(M9), and cross-case linking and ringleader identification (M10).

Of {{m8_m10.N}} measurable records,
{{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.subject_victim.n}} identify a victim or
complainant as an interview subject,
{{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.subject_member.n}} an ordinary member and
{{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.subject_leader.n}} a leader. Confession
reliability is discussed in {{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.reliability.n}}
records, while interrogation method — information-gathering or accusatorial — is
named in {{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.methods_information.n}} and
{{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.methods_accusatorial.n}} records
respectively. In M9, online recruitment appears in
{{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.recruitment_online.n}} records,
organisational structure read from digital traces in
{{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.structure_link.n}}, financial records
in {{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.financial.n}}, and communications
evidence and seized devices in
{{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.communications.n}} and
{{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.devices.n}}. In M10, modus-operandi
patterning appears in {{m8_m10.modules.M10_case_linking.modus_operandi.n}}
records and cross-jurisdiction linking in
{{m8_m10.modules.M10_case_linking.cross_jurisdiction.n}}; ringleader
identification appears in {{m8_m10.modules.M10_case_linking.ringleader_id.n}}
and victim identification in {{m8_m10.modules.M10_case_linking.victim_id.n}}.

Two readings are possible and the corpus cannot distinguish them. Either the
investigative practice is not described in the published literature — plausible,
given that operational detail is rarely publishable — or it is described and the
abstract does not carry it. Both readings support the same recommendation, because
the reporting set is addressed to abstracts, but the distinction matters for the
claim: what is thin here is the *published trace* of investigative practice, not
evidence that practice is absent.

The coding itself carries a limitation worth stating. These modules were specified
as manual coding over full texts; this run had abstracts only, and the coding was
done by an instrument-assisted procedure with every code and its triggering span
recorded per record. The authors' review of those spans is what would convert them
from assisted to manual codes.

### 6.3 What the sensitivity analyses change

None of the five analyses moves the headline. E1 shows that the automatic-exclusion
rule is insensitive to its threshold across the range 0.60 to 0.90, not because the
rule is robust but because the model's stated confidence clusters at 0.90 and 0.95;
a threshold is not a graded dial when the dial has two positions. E2 is the most
informative result for the method: under the 14B layer the lexical half of the group
criterion adds {{experiments.E2_i1_conjunction.extra_if_disjunctive}} records beyond
what the model's own I1 verdict removes, so the gate that mattered for the 7B layer
is redundant at 14B. That is a statement about where the fix belongs — in the
prompt, not in a post-hoc rule — and it is reported rather than quietly kept as
belt-and-braces. E3 shows that
{{experiments.E3_source_contribution.source_exclusive_records.s2}} records are unique
to Semantic Scholar, {{experiments.E3_source_contribution.source_exclusive_records.core}}
to CORE and {{experiments.E3_source_contribution.source_exclusive_records.crossref}}
to Crossref, so the corpus is not one source wearing six names. E4 records what the
year and language limits removed. E5 compares the two screening layers: agreement on
the decision is {{e5.decision.agree_pct}} per cent with a kappa of {{e5.decision.kappa}},
which is substantial but far from interchangeable, and the layers agree on INCLUDE
for {{e5.include_counts.14B}} of the fifty sampled records.

Two further diagnostics belong here. An embedding pass over the eligible records
(bge-m3, cosine on title and abstract) found
{{embed_dedup_summary.clusters_095}} near-duplicate clusters at a threshold of 0.95,
including the same paper in two languages and the same record served by two
sources; they are listed for the authors rather than merged automatically, because
two of the clusters join works that are related but distinct. The overall reading is
that the corpus is source-dependent in composition but not in conclusion: adding
three sources multiplied the included set severalfold and left the reporting profile
in Figure 3 unchanged in shape.
