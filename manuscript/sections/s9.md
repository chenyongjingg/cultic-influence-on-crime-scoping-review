Three questions follow from the map. Each is stated in a form that a future
literature could settle, and each is left without a formal falsification design
because the design would be an empirical study, not a review.

**Q1. Does role-graded reporting change the consistency of mechanism–crime
findings?** If records began to classify participants by role and duration, the
same mechanism should be associated with different outcomes at different levels,
and the association should look more consistent across studies than it does now.
The prediction is testable with the same corpus re-read in five years: the
between-study variance in what a mechanism is said to cause should fall when role
is held constant.

**Q2. Does adoption of a reporting set precede or follow empirical progress?** If
the set is adopted, it should appear first in the studies that already have data —
it is cheap to report what one has measured — and only later change what is
measured. The prediction is that adoption of MRS-CI will be visible before any
change in the underlying instruments, and that instrument development will follow
adoption rather than cause it.

**Q3. Is the legal outcome the binding constraint?** If the interface is where the
literature fails — mechanism reported, outcome not — then studies that do report a
legal outcome should be disproportionately those built from court files, and
studies built from interviews should almost never follow a case to judgment. The
prediction is a strong association between data-source type and whether a legal
outcome is reported, which the current corpus suggests but is too small to
establish.

Each question shares a property worth naming: it can be answered by counting, not
by arguing. That is the same property the review itself relied on, and it is the
reason the recommendation is a reporting set rather than a theory. A theory of
cultic influence on crime is not what this literature lacks; it has several. What
it lacks is the ability to tell which of them the data support.
