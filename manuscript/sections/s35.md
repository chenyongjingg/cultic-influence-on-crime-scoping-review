The research topic asks about forensic implications, and behind them sits a question
about what a state can actually do. Two layers of practice lie behind the reporting
profile, and the corpus lets us count how visible each one is.

The first half of this section reports the investigative modules M8–M10 that
Section 3.4 introduced; the second half reports the regulatory layer and reads both
in governance terms.

**The investigative layer.** Of {{m8_m10.N}} measurable records,
{{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.subject_victim.n}} identify a victim or complainant
as an interview subject, {{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.subject_member.n}} an
ordinary member and {{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.subject_leader.n}} a leader.
Confession reliability is discussed in
{{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.reliability.n}} records. Interrogation method — whether
information-gathering or accusatorial — is named in
{{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.methods_information.n}} and
{{m8_m10.modules.M8_interrogation.methods_accusatorial.n}} records respectively, which
is a striking absence given how well established the effect of method on true and false
confessions is {meissner,kassin} and how unreliable demeanour-based deception detection has proved
{vrij}. In the digital layer, online recruitment appears in
{{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.recruitment_online.n}} records and organisational
structure inferred from digital traces in
{{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.structure_link.n}}; financial records appear in
{{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.financial.n}}, and communications evidence and
seized devices in {{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.communications.n}} and
{{m8_m10.modules.M9_digital_forensics.devices.n}}. Case-linking appears as

These are gap findings, not judgements of capability. Investigative practice exists in
case files, and case files do not enter the searchable literature; the counts show an
absent public trace, not an absent practice. This is Section 3.4.1 seen from the other
side: there the information was in the paper and not in the abstract, here the practice
is in the file and not in the paper.

**The regulatory layer.** Regulatory instruments are named even less often. Of
{{regulatory_tools.N}} records, {{regulatory_tools.any_instrument_named}}
({{regulatory_tools.any_instrument_pct}}%) name any instrument of this kind, and only
{{regulatory_tools.two_or_more_named}} name more than one. Criminal prosecution
dominates the small set that is named
({{regulatory_tools.counts.criminal_prosecution.n}}, {{regulatory_tools.counts.criminal_prosecution.pct}}%),
followed by immigration law
({{regulatory_tools.counts.immigration_law.n}}), trafficking law
({{regulatory_tools.counts.trafficking_law.n}}), family and marriage law
({{regulatory_tools.counts.family_marriage_law.n}}) and financial regulation
({{regulatory_tools.counts.financial_consumer_regulation.n}}); charity and religious-organisation
regulation and civil commitment are named in none. Where an instrument does appear it is
usually the one that produced the case before the court — racketeering prosecutions and
civil suits against new religious movements {mihalyfy}, the application of trafficking law to
high-control groups together with proposed amendments {boylelaisure}, and sentencing principles in
cult-related cases {wang}.

That distribution is a governance finding in its own right. The literature treats
regulation as what happens to a group once an offence is established, and almost never
as an instrument operating earlier, on information. MRS-CI is best understood in those terms: an
information-disclosure-based, low-burden regulatory instrument. It does not license,
prohibit or sanction; it changes what must be made visible, so that otherwise
incomparable case narratives become an auditable and comparable record. The mechanism
is the same one used in risk-assessment registration and in algorithmic accountability:
a disclosure obligation imposed at the point where an organisation already holds the
information, converting unverifiable narrative into a record that a regulator, a court
or a later study can check. This is Section 4.2's "cheapest possible intervention" said
in regulatory rather than methodological terms.

Read against the topic's three themes: manipulation technique is what items 4 and 5
make countable, group dynamics is what items 2 and 3 make comparable, and forensic
implication is what items 6 to 9 place in a form a court can weigh. The investigative
counts show which of those the literature currently makes available to a fact-finder,
and the regulatory counts show which it makes available to a regulator — in both cases,
the answer for this corpus is: fewer than the underlying cases contain.

investigative guide, not a legislative proposal, and it draws on no unpublished or
restricted case material.
